# Indian Banking and Judicial Systems — Complete Hierarchy

India-wide structure • Maharashtra examples • Full forms • Posts and authorities • Roles and powers • Tenure • Approximate institutional coverage • Customer and citizen access

We can extend the same hierarchy framework to two more systems:

- Banking system: Who regulates banks, who owns them, how head offices and branches are organised, who controls lending and complaints, and how a citizen accesses banking services.
- Judicial system: Who interprets the law, how courts are arranged from the Supreme Court to local courts, who appoints judges, how appeals work, and where a citizen files a case.

The most important distinction is that the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) regulates banks, but it does not directly manage every bank or decide every customer's loan. Likewise, the Supreme Court is India's highest court, but it does not ordinarily hear every case first. Most disputes begin in a court or tribunal with jurisdiction over the subject and location.&#x20;

# BOARD 1 — THE INDIAN BANKING SYSTEM

## 1. Complete banking hierarchy: from national institutions to customers

## PEOPLE, BUSINESSES AND THE ECONOMY

Depositors • Borrowers • Businesses • Government • Investors

### A. Central banking and regulation

Reserve Bank of India (RBI) → banking regulation, monetary policy, supervision, currency issuance and payment-system oversight within its legal mandate.

### B. Government policy and ownership

Ministry of Finance → Department of Financial Services (DFS) → government policy, public-sector bank ownership matters and financial-inclusion programmes.

This is a parallel government-policy/ownership channel, not the RBI's ordinary management chain.

Commercial banks

Public-sector, private-sector, foreign banks and Regional Rural Banks

Other banking institutions

Co-operative banks, small finance banks and payments banks

### C. Individual bank's organisational structure

Board of Directors → Managing Director / Chief Executive Officer → corporate/head office → circles, zones or regional offices → branches and service channels.

### D. Customers

Individuals • Families • Farmers • Small businesses • Corporates • Public institutions

How this differs from democracy: banking is not a direct electoral hierarchy. Bank leaders are appointed through governance procedures, bank employees are hired, and regulators exercise statutory powers. Customers choose banks and have legal rights, but they do not elect a bank's branch manager.

### How many people does a bank branch represent?

Unlike an MLA or councillor, a branch manager does not represent a fixed population. A branch serves a customer base and catchment area, which may overlap with other branches and digital channels.

For illustration:

| Type of branch or banking outlet  | Illustrative service population                                      |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Small rural branch                | 2,000–15,000 people                                                  |
| Larger rural or semi-urban branch | 10,000–50,000 people                                                 |
| Urban retail branch               | 10,000–1,00,000+ people                                              |
| Major metropolitan branch         | Can serve a much larger customer base, including non-local customers |

These are illustrative ranges, not RBI standards or official branch-to-population ratios. A branch may serve tens of thousands of account holders, while digital banking can serve customers far outside its physical catchment.

## 5. How banking decisions are made

The bank's internal approval hierarchy matters because a branch manager cannot necessarily approve every loan.

Customer submits application

Branch / relationship team checks documents

Credit assessment: income, repayment capacity, security and risk

Appropriate sanctioning authority or credit committee

Documentation, conditions, disbursement and monitoring

Who has final approval? It depends on the loan type, amount, collateral, risk category and the bank's delegation-of-powers policy. A small retail loan might be approved at branch or centralised processing level; a large corporate loan may require higher-level credit committees.

RBI sets prudential and regulatory requirements. The individual bank applies those requirements through its credit policy and approval system.

### If a bank has done something wrong

A practical complaint route is:

1. Bank branch or customer-care channel: Raise the issue and retain the complaint reference.
2. Bank's grievance redressal / nodal officer: Escalate if the initial response is inadequate.
3. RBI Complaint Management System: For eligible complaints under the RBI Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, after satisfying the applicable conditions.
4. Consumer commission, civil court, police or another competent authority: Where the issue falls within that institution's jurisdiction. A fraud allegation or contractual dispute may require a different route.

A complaint to RBI does not guarantee a refund or acceptance of the customer's claim; the outcome depends on the facts and applicable rules.

# BOARD 2 — THE INDIAN JUDICIAL SYSTEM

## 7. Complete judicial hierarchy: from the Supreme Court to citizens

India's formal court structure is headed by the Supreme Court of India, followed by High Courts and subordinate courts. Specialised tribunals operate alongside the regular court system under specific laws. Gram Nyayalayas also exist under a separate statutory framework where established.&#x20;

## CITIZENS, BUSINESSES AND THE STATE

People seeking remedies, defending rights or resolving disputes

### A. Supreme Court of India

Chief Justice of India (CJI) + other Supreme Court judges.

Highest court; constitutional cases, appeals and other matters within its jurisdiction.

### B. High Courts

Chief Justice of the High Court + other judges; one High Court can cover one or more states/UTs.

Constitutional writ jurisdiction, appeals, supervisory jurisdiction and other original/appellate work.

### C. District and Sessions Courts

District Judge / Principal District Judge and Sessions Judge, with additional judges and subordinate courts.

Major civil and criminal jurisdiction at district level, subject to statutory allocation.

Civil courts

Civil Judge (Junior Division) → Civil Judge (Senior Division) and other designated civil courts, according to state structure.

Criminal courts

Judicial Magistrate courts → Chief Judicial Magistrate / Chief Metropolitan Magistrate and Sessions Courts, as applicable.

### D. Specialised and alternative forums

Family Courts • Commercial Courts • Consumer Commissions • Debt Recovery Tribunals • National Company Law Tribunal • Labour Courts/Industrial Tribunals • Gram Nyayalayas • Lok Adalats and mediation.

Important: The arrows are conceptual, not a universal appeal route. A case does not automatically travel from the lowest court to the Supreme Court. Appeals, revisions, writ petitions and transfers are governed by the applicable law, subject matter, territorial jurisdiction and procedural requirements.

### What powers does the Supreme Court have?

- Interprets the Constitution and decides constitutional questions within its jurisdiction.
- Hears specified appeals from High Courts, tribunals and other courts.
- Exercises original jurisdiction in specified federal disputes.
- Can issue constitutional remedies under Article 32 for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
- Can review its judgments within the permitted legal framework.
- Its law declared under Article 141 is binding on all courts in India.

The Supreme Court is not a ministry. Neither the Prime Minister nor an ordinary government department can simply direct a judge how to decide a case.

## 12. How judges are appointed, and who can remove them?

Judges are not elected in the same way as MPs or MLAs. Their appointment and independence are governed by the Constitution, judicial appointment procedures and applicable service rules.

| Judicial post                     | How appointed                                                                                                | Normal tenure / retirement                                |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Chief Justice of India            | Appointed by the President under the constitutional appointment framework                                    | Until age 65                                              |
| Supreme Court Judge               | Appointed by the President through the prevailing judicial appointment process                               | Until age 65                                              |
| High Court Chief Justice / Judge  | Appointed by the President through the prevailing judicial appointment process                               | Until age 62                                              |
| District Judge                    | Appointed by the Governor in consultation with the High Court under Article 233, subject to applicable rules | Service-based; retirement rules apply                     |
| Civil Judge / Judicial Magistrate | Selected through the relevant state judicial-service recruitment and appointment process                     | Service-based; rules vary by state and cadre              |
| Tribunal member / judge           | Appointed under the statute governing that tribunal                                                          | Depends on the tribunal's governing law and service rules |

The Supreme Court and High Court judges have constitutionally protected removal procedures. They cannot simply be dismissed by a minister because of an unfavourable judgment. For Supreme Court and High Court judges, removal requires the prescribed parliamentary process on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity, followed by the President's order.&#x20;

### Who does what in judicial administration?

- Chief Justice of India: Leads the Supreme Court institutionally and performs judicial and administrative responsibilities.
- Chief Justice of a High Court: Leads the High Court and performs administrative functions relating to that court and the subordinate judiciary under the applicable framework.
- District Judge / Principal District Judge: Performs judicial work and assigned district-court administrative functions.
- State government: Has defined roles in judicial infrastructure, budgeting and judicial-service matters, subject to constitutional safeguards and judicial independence.
- Department of Justice, Ministry of Law and Justice: Handles Union-level justice-policy and administration matters assigned to it.
- National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC): Parliament enacted an NJAC framework in 2014, but the Supreme Court struck down the constitutional amendment and associated Act in 2015. The prevailing judicial appointment system must therefore be understood in light of that judgment, not by assuming the NJAC Act governs current appointments.&#x20;

## 13. How many people does each court represent?

This is different from political representation. A judge does not represent a fixed population in the way an MP represents a constituency.

| Institution                | Geographic coverage                                          | Approximate population / workload                                                              |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Supreme Court of India     | Entire country                                               | National-level jurisdiction; hears matters admitted or assigned under its jurisdiction         |
| High Court                 | One state or multiple states/UTs                             | State or regional jurisdiction; workload depends on filings, appeals and original jurisdiction |
| District Court             | District or assigned judicial district                       | Population varies substantially; workload depends on case type, filing rates and jurisdiction  |
| Taluka / subordinate court | Defined local judicial jurisdiction                          | Often serves a smaller geographical area, but no universal population ratio                    |
| Family Court               | Designated local jurisdiction                                | Workload depends on family-dispute filings and court allocation                                |
| Consumer Commission        | District, state or national jurisdiction, depending on level | Workload depends on eligible consumer complaints and statutory jurisdiction                    |
| DRT                        | Not necessarily confined to one ordinary district            | Handles specified financial recovery matters within its assigned territorial jurisdiction      |
| Lok Adalat                 | Local or institutional jurisdiction as arranged              | Handles eligible cases and settlements, rather than representing a fixed population            |

A useful judicial-capacity measure is:

\\[ \text{Pending cases per judge} = \frac{\text{Cases pending in the relevant court}}{\text{Judges available to hear them}} \\]

Even this figure must be interpreted carefully because cases differ greatly in complexity, duration and procedural stage. A judge may hear hundreds of matters on a cause list, but that does not mean every matter receives a final judgment that day.

For official case pendency, court-wise strength and disposal statistics, use the National Judicial Data Grid and the relevant court's published data. The Supreme Court of India provides the national court hierarchy and judicial information on its [official website](https://www.sci.gov.in/).

## 14. How a citizen's case moves through the judicial system

The correct route depends on whether the issue is civil, criminal, constitutional, commercial, family-related, a consumer dispute or another specialised matter.

Citizen identifies a legal dispute

Property • Contract • Crime • Employment • Family • Consumer • Constitutional rights

Step 1 — Determine the correct forum

Ordinary court, tribunal, consumer commission, statutory authority or alternative dispute-resolution process.

Step 2 — File the case or complaint

Meet the jurisdiction, limitation, fee, documentation and procedural requirements.

Step 3 — Hearings and evidence

The court or tribunal considers pleadings, evidence, arguments and applicable law.

Step 4 — Order or judgment

The competent authority issues a decision, order, decree, conviction, acquittal or other legally available remedy.

Step 5 — Appeal, review or execution, if permitted

The relevant law determines whether an appeal or review is available and which court or tribunal can hear it.

For example, a property dispute may begin in a competent civil court; a serious criminal trial may proceed through a Sessions Court; a constitutional challenge may reach a High Court or the Supreme Court through the appropriate jurisdiction; and a specified bank-debt recovery case may fall within a Debt Recovery Tribunal's jurisdiction.

There is no universal right to appeal every order to every higher court. Limitation periods and procedural rules matter.

| Does each post represent a fixed population? | No; banks serve customer bases and geographic markets                                                                                  | No; courts have jurisdiction and caseloads, not political constituencies                       |

| Who provides frontline service?              | Bank branch, customer service, digital banking and other authorised channels                                                           | Court registry, filing counters, legal services authorities and the relevant court or tribunal |

## 16. A combined mental map

## Three different kinds of authority

Democracy

Citizens → elected representatives → government policy and public administration

Banking

Regulators → bank governance → operations → customers

Judiciary

Supreme Court → High Courts → subordinate courts, with statutory tribunals alongside

These are different systems, not a single chain of command. The RBI does not sit above the Supreme Court, and the Supreme Court does not run bank branches. Each institution acts under its own legal authority.
